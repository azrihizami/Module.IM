How a magnet rotor is made

How the permanent-magnet rotor of an EV traction motor is made. From rare-earth oxide to a sintered, coated magnet, then stack, insert, fix, skew, balance and magnetise: every step of the rotor on our own parametric 3D model, which you can turn, cut open, play as a build sequence and redesign. Below it, the steps in order, with the numbers behind them linked to their sources.

What it is, and why it is hard

Most EV traction motors are permanent-magnet synchronous motors with an interior-magnet rotor: a laminated steel core with pockets, and a rare-earth magnet in each pocket. The magnets give the field for free, with no rotor current and no rotor copper loss, which is why these motors are hard to beat at the light loads where a car spends most of its time. Sitting inside the steel, the magnets are held at speed, and the steel paths around them add reluctance torque.

Neodymium-iron-boron is used because no other affordable magnet comes close in energy. Its weak points are heat and supply. Hot NdFeB loses coercivity fast, and a motor's worst short circuit at its highest temperature must not demagnetise it; that needs dysprosium or terbium, the scarcest of the elements. Ferrite is cheap and has no rare earth, but it is much weaker and loses its remanence faster with heat. Designers have learned to use less: one hybrid motor went from 1.2 kg of magnet in 2004 to about 0.5 kg in 2017.

Making the rotor is a chain of very different trades: chemistry and metallurgy for the magnet, precision stamping, moulding and balancing for the rotor, high-current pulse work for magnetising. Each one is a skill, and a supplier, that a motor industry needs.

Lynas Malaysia has separated rare earths at Gebeng since 2012, and in 2025 it signed for mixed rare-earth carbonate from Kelantan and became the first commercial producer of separated heavy rare earths outside China, with dysprosium oxide in May and terbium oxide in June. Downstream, Shin-Etsu has made rare-earth magnets in Malaysia since 1988 and Bomatec makes bonded magnets and magnet assemblies, both largely on imported processed metals.

On 7 July 2026 Lynas and the Korean magnet maker JS Link agreed to build a sintered-magnet plant in the Gebeng Industrial Estate: construction from the fourth quarter of 2026, commissioning targeted for the fourth quarter of 2027, with Lynas supplying it exclusively until January 2038. The national rare-earth roadmap, tabled at the National Economic Action Council, aims at this downstream step; a policy study estimates that a fully integrated chain could add RM91.9 billion to GDP and 96,900 jobs by 2050. The industry is also debated here, particularly over environmental concerns.

A magnet plant needs people who can run and check each step in the diagram, and a motor industry needs suppliers who can turn magnets into rotors: stamping, moulding, magnetising, balancing and testing. This module is written for them, and for the engineers who specify and buy the parts.

The build, step by step

From rare-earth oxide to a rotor in the stator

Thirteen steps. Steps 01 to 03 make the magnets, 04 to 10 build the rotor, 11 to 13 prove it and put it in the motor. Eight are shown on our parametric model, with one still from the viewer each; the five that happen away from the rotor line have a diagram. The stills show the 8-pole EV rotor: Ø132.5 × 120 mm, 16 magnets of 17 × 4.5 mm in a 122° V, 3-step skew.

01Away from the rotor line · diagram

From oxide to alloy

A magnet starts as separated rare-earth oxide: NdPr for the bulk of the magnet, dysprosium or terbium for heat resistance. In Malaysia the oxides are made at Gebeng, near Kuantan, in a plant with three areas: cracking and leaching, solvent extraction and product finishing. The oxide is reduced to metal by molten-salt electrolysis: the standard cell dissolves the oxide in a neodymium fluoride and lithium fluoride salt at about 1050 °C, and liquid metal collects at the cathode.

The metal is melted under vacuum with iron, ferro-boron and small additions (Dy or Tb, cobalt, aluminium, copper, gallium) and poured onto a water-cooled copper wheel. This strip casting freezes the alloy in flakes 200 to 400 µm thick with a fine grain, and leaves no time for free iron to form. The metal and alloy steps are the gap in the Malaysian chain today: the magnet makers here largely depend on imported processed metals.

02Away from the rotor line · diagram

Powder, press, sinter

The flakes take up hydrogen in the grain-boundary phase and crack into coarse powder under 2 mm. A jet mill, where the particles collide in streams of nitrogen, grinds it to 2 to 5 µm, close to one grain per particle. The powder is weighed into a die, turned by a magnetic field so that every grain's easy axis lines up, and pressed; the green block has about 69 % of the final density.

Vacuum sintering at 1000 to 1100 °C closes the pores and shrinks the block to its final density; two annealing stages, 850 to 950 °C and 400 to 600 °C, set the coercivity. The blocks leave the furnace unmagnetised, but with their direction of magnetisation fixed by the pressing field.

03Away from the rotor line · diagram

Cut, diffuse, coat, check

The blocks are sliced and ground to the magnet size, and this is where material is lost: machining generates up to 30 % swarf and scrap, which goes to recycling. For traction magnets the next step matters most. Dysprosium or terbium raises the coercivity that a hot motor needs; instead of alloying it through the whole magnet, it is put on the surface and diffused in along the grain boundaries. In one study, 0.4 % terbium by weight gave 1014 kA/m of coercivity at 120 °C, with remanence only falling from 1.45 to 1.43 T; an earlier dysprosium process needed 81 % less dysprosium for the same performance.

Sintered NdFeB corrodes in air, so every magnet is coated: a nickel-copper-nickel stack of about 10 to 20 µm, or epoxy. The coating counts in the fit: on a dimension it adds twice its thickness. Inspection measures remanence and coercivity on a hysteresigraph (IEC 60404-5) and checks the flux of each piece with a Helmholtz coil and fluxmeter.

04On our model

Stack the core segments

The rotor core is a stack of electrical-steel laminations, punched with the magnet pockets. Each pole has a V of two pockets; between the pockets and the rotor surface only a thin bridge of steel remains, and a rib joins the two halves of the V. Both are compromises: thin bridges and ribs leak less magnet flux, thick ones hold the magnets at speed.

Our EV rotor is Ø132.5 × 120 mm, sized to the 48-slot hairpin stator with a 0.75 mm air gap. Its bridges are 1.2 mm, the centre ribs 1.6 mm, and 3.8 mm of steel stays between neighbouring poles. It is stacked as 3 packets of 40 mm, one for each skew step, with eight Ø17 mm holes to take mass out where the flux does not go.

05On our model

Insert the magnets

The magnets go in axially, one piece after another, and usually unmagnetised: an unmagnetised piece does not jump to the steel, does not collect chips, and does not mind the heat of the next step. On our rotor each pocket of each packet takes 2 pieces, 17.0 × 4.5 × 19.6 mm: 96 pieces and 1.1 kg of NdFeB in all.

The pieces are short on purpose. The inverter's harmonics induce eddy currents in a conducting magnet, and cutting it raises the resistance of their path (segmentation increases the effective resistance of the eddy-current loop). One 80 kW traction design used six magnet segments along the axis for this reason.

06On our model

Fix the magnets

A loose magnet rattles and wears; at speed it is pressed outward against the bridge. Adhesive alone can let the magnet move, shed flakes, or lose its hold above its glass-transition temperature. Many traction rotors therefore inject resin, or transfer-mould an epoxy compound, into the gap around each magnet. Moulding compounds are processed at around 175 °C, one more reason the magnets go in unmagnetised and are magnetised after the rotor is assembled. The cost is resin and time: injection and curing lengthen the manufacturing time.

Our model leaves 0.2 mm of clearance around each magnet and fills it, with the empty end barriers of the pocket, with resin.

07On our model

Stack on the shaft with a step skew

The packets go onto the keyed shaft, each turned a little from the last. A rotor cannot be skewed smoothly like a cast induction rotor, because the magnets are straight blocks, so it is skewed in steps. Step skew is very effective against cogging torque; what it does to torque ripple under load depends on the operating point and has to be checked.

With 48 slots and 8 poles the cogging torque repeats every 7.5° (360° over the least common multiple of 48 and 8). Our rotor uses 3 steps of 2.5°: 5° in all, two-thirds of that period. The lecture R5 compares one, two and three steps in FEMM.

08On our model

Fit the end plates

End plates of non-magnetic stainless steel or aluminium close the stack, hold it axially and keep the resin and magnets in. They are also where the balancing correction goes. On our model they are 5 mm thick and stop 2 mm short of the rotor surface.

09On our model

Balance

The rotor is spun on a balancing machine and the unbalance is corrected in two planes, here by drilling the end plates. Standard electric motors are balanced to grade G2.5 of ISO 21940-11. The grade is a speed in mm/s, so the allowed unbalance per kilogram is the grade divided by the angular speed: for a 10 kg rotor running at 12,000 rpm (1257 rad/s) that is 2.0 g·mm per kilogram, 20 g·mm in all. Faster rotors must be balanced finer.

10On our model

Magnetise

The assembled rotor goes into a magnetising fixture with one pole shoe and coil per rotor pole, and a capacitor bank discharges through the coils. The field must be strong enough to saturate the magnet, several times its coercivity, deep inside the rotor and through the steel. This has been shown for exactly our kind of rotor: a 50 kW, 8-pole, 48-slot V-type rotor for an EV, magnetised after assembly in a fixture designed for it.

Ferrite magnets need far less field and can even be magnetised by the motor's own winding; for NdFeB in an interior rotor, the separate fixture is the proper method.

11Away from the rotor line · diagram

Spin test

Rotors of a new design, and samples from production, are spun above the normal operating ceiling in a containment chamber, typically in a vacuum of about 1 mbar. The diameter and the balance are measured before and after the run: a bridge that yielded shows as a larger diameter, a magnet that moved as a change in balance. The air gap of our rotor is 0.75 mm, so a growth of a few hundredths of a millimetre already matters.

12Away from the rotor line · diagram

Check the rotor

Before it meets a stator, the magnetised rotor is checked pole by pole: a Hall probe close to the surface, the rotor turning on a rotary table, gives the distribution of the field around the rotor. A missing, weak or reversed magnet shows at once. The lecture R6 adds the back-EMF check in a reference stator on our model.

13On our model

Into the stator

A magnetised rotor pulls hard towards the stator steel, so it is never pushed in by hand: a guide holds it on the axis and lowers it into the bore, and a guided fixture keeps it off the teeth until the bearings take over. Magnetising the parts before assembly makes this, and keeping ferrous debris out, the hard part of the line. The viewer ends here, with our rotor in the stator of the hairpin article.

Questions

Magnet rotors: short answers

What is an interior permanent magnet rotor?

A rotor whose magnets sit in pockets inside the laminated steel core instead of on its surface. The steel holds them at speed, and the different paths for flux along and across the poles add reluctance torque to the magnet torque. Most EV traction motors use one or two V-shaped layers of magnets per pole.

Why are the magnets inserted unmagnetised?

Unmagnetised pieces do not jump to the steel or collect chips, and they survive the heat of fixing: moulding compounds are processed at around 175 °C. The whole rotor is then magnetised in a fixture, which has been shown on a 50 kW, 8-pole V-type EV rotor.

What do dysprosium and terbium do in a traction magnet?

They raise the coercivity, the resistance to demagnetisation, which NdFeB loses quickly as it warms: about 0.4 to 0.6 % per °C. Diffusing them in from the surface puts them where they work: 0.4 % terbium by weight gave 1014 kA/m at 120 °C in one study.

Why are the magnets cut into pieces?

To cut eddy-current loss. The inverter's harmonics induce currents in the conducting magnet; short pieces force those currents into longer, higher-resistance paths (segmentation), so the magnet runs cooler. One 80 kW design used six pieces along the axis.

Can a traction motor do without rare-earth magnets?

Yes, at a cost in size or efficiency: induction and synchronous reluctance motors use no magnets, and ferrite costs little but is much weaker and loses its remanence faster with heat (−0.20 %/°C, against about −0.12 %/°C for NdFeB). Designs have also learned to use less: one hybrid's motor went from 1.2 kg of magnet in 2004 to about 0.5 kg in 2017.
