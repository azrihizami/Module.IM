    0:00Impregnate and test

    Impregnate and test. Lecture five of the Manufacturing module. Lecture four left our EV-size stator, 48 slots and 8 poles, welded and coated: 102 joints. Now it is sealed in resin, and then it must prove it works. The steps: heat, trickle, gel and cure. Then the tests: resistance, insulation resistance, the high-voltage test called hipot, surge and partial discharge. And a decision on any stator that fails. The resistances, voltages and resin volumes here come from our Hairpin Motor Designer, run on that stator.

    0:46Why resin

    Chapter one. Why resin. Look inside one slot. Our 3.6-millimetre bars leave 0.15 millimetres on each side, between bar and liner. With the gaps between the layers, 12 percent of the slot is still air. Resin fills that air, for three reasons. First, it glues the pins to each other and to the liner, so vibration does not rub the enamel or work on the welds. Second, heat. The copper's heat must cross into the steel. Air conducts about 0.026 watts per metre-kelvin; an impregnating resin about 0.21, eight times more. Between copper and steel lie the enamel, that gap and the liner. In our slot the air gap holds two thirds of their thermal resistance. Fill it with resin and the three resist less than half as much: at 10 amps per square millimetre, the temperature drop falls from 33 kelvin to 13, in our one-dimensional estimate. Third, protection: resin keeps out moisture and oil, and removes the air where small sparks, partial discharges, can start.

    2:02Trickle and cure

    Chapter two. Trickle and cure. The resins are polyesterimides, polyesters or epoxies that harden with heat. Viscosity, the resistance to flow, decides the process: one resin sold for EV motors is like honey at room temperature, about 2,200 millipascal-seconds, and like oil at 80 degrees, about 100. So the stator is heated first. Dipping the stator in a tank wastes resin and leaves it where it is not wanted; vacuum-pressure impregnation, VPI, suits large machines. For car motors, one study calls trickling the usual method. Trickling: the hot stator turns slowly on a shaft, tilted a few degrees, while nozzles drip resin onto both end windings. Capillary action, the pull that draws liquid into a thin gap, carries it into the slots. On an eight-layer, 48-slot stator, one study timed 35 minutes to the far end; simulated without capillary forces, no resin arrived in 45. In our slot, resin fills the side gaps, the gaps between layers and the corners: 11 square millimetres. Over the 120-millimetre stack and 48 slots, that is 64 cubic centimetres, about 75 grams. A film on the end windings could add half as much again. Only as much is dripped as the winding takes, so the bore stays clean. The stator is weighed before and after. The resin gels, thickening to a jelly, while the stator keeps turning so none runs out, then cures: for that resin, 15 to 30 minutes at 130 degrees. Some lines coat the weld heads only now.

    4:02Resistance

    Chapter three. Resistance. A four-wire meter drives about one amp through two of the stator's leads and measures the voltage with a second pair of probes, so contact resistance does not count. Resistance is rho, the resistivity, times length over area. For copper at 20 degrees, rho is one fifty-eighth of an ohm for one metre of one square millimetre. One path of our winding is 7.49 metres of 14.15 square millimetres: 9.12 milliohms. Its 17 joints bring it to 9.17. Each phase has two paths in parallel: 4.59 milliohms. The star point is inside the stator, so the meter sees two phases in series. Between U and V: 9.17 milliohms again. Copper's resistance rises about 0.4 percent per kelvin: at 150 degrees our phase is 6.93 milliohms. So the tester measures the temperature and corrects every reading to 20 degrees. The three readings, U to V, V to W and W to U, must agree with each other and the design, within a window the maker sets. A missing weld opens one path: that phase doubles, and two readings jump by half. A poor weld is another story. In lecture four, a gapped joint measured five micro-ohms more than a good one. Shared with the parallel path, that lifts the U to V reading by 1.2 micro-ohms, 0.013 percent, as much as a temperature error of four hundredths of a kelvin. The meter could resolve it; no temperature correction can. So every weld was checked as it was made.

    5:58Insulation under voltage

    Chapter four. Insulation under voltage. Three boundaries must hold: phase to core, phase to phase, and turn to turn. First, what an 800-volt motor sees. The 800 volts is the battery's DC voltage, the DC bus. The inverter turns it into AC: between two leads, the line voltage reaches at most about 566 volts r.m.s., and its peaks touch the full 800. Our model knows each conductor's place in its path, so it maps the voltage between neighbours at that full voltage, without switching spikes. Here is the surprise: our crown spans five slots, not six, so the even layers sit one slot over. Half the neighbouring pairs in the slots, 72 of 144, are two different phases, with only enamel between them. The worst pair, the first conductors after the leads, sees 97 percent of the line peak: about 780 volts on an 800-volt bus, before spikes. With a six-slot crown, no slot would hold two phases. Is that unusual? No. Every two-layer winding with a shortened crown has slots shared by two phases. Makers keep the short pitch because it cuts harmonics, and arrange the connections so the conductors next to the leads never meet; patents describe exactly that. In our stator only 3 of the 72 pairs exceed 90 percent of the line peak, and the average is about half. Test one: insulation resistance. 500 or 1,000 volts DC go between winding and core; one standard asks at least five megohms, and one maker of test gear sees up to ten gigaohms on new parts. Test two: the hipot, short for high potential, the withstand test. The three leads are joined, the core is grounded, and an AC voltage far above the working voltage is applied, so weak insulation fails here, not in the car. The machine standard sets twice the rated voltage plus 1,000 volts, for a minute, or, in series production, 120 percent for one second. Nothing may break down. The standard's U is the motor's AC line voltage, so for an 800-volt battery U is 566 volts: 2.1 kilovolts for a minute, or 2.6 for a second. Taking the full 800 instead, a stricter reading, gives 2.6 and 3.1. Our neutral ring joins the phases, so this test, like insulation resistance, checks winding to core, not phase to phase. Test three: surge. A capacitor discharges into two phases and the winding rings like a bell; all three pairs should ring alike. A shorted turn rings faster and dies sooner. But our path has only 16 turns. A common surge level, twice the bus voltage plus 1,000 volts, is 2.6 kilovolts. Shared evenly over two phases, each turn gets about 80 volts, below the roughly 350 any air gap needs to spark. So surge finds shorts and wrong connections, not weak enamel between one turn and the next. Test four: partial discharge, small sparks in air gaps that do not bridge the insulation but slowly eat the enamel. The tester raises a voltage between leads, or to the core, and listens for the first discharges: the inception voltage. An 800-volt traction motor is designed to stay free of them. One European project set the limit between phases at 1,920 volts peak to peak: twice the bus voltage, plus 20 percent for spikes. Our worst slot pair swings 1,550 before spikes. That project found 110-micrometre enamel enough at 180 degrees, and 80 not. Ours is 120. All these run before the resin, when some faults can still be fixed, and again after.

    10:28Find and reject

    Chapter five. Find and reject. Each test has its faults. Resistance finds open paths; insulation resistance and hipot, weak insulation to the core; surge, shorted turns and wrong connections; partial discharge, thin enamel or bars too close. Did the resin get in? The scale says how much. Our model suggests a second clue: if the gaps fill, the capacitance to the core rises by half, from 6.9 to 10.6 nanofarads. A bad weld caught before the heads are coated can be repaired. One patent cuts it out and joins the ends by arc welding with filler, as a second laser weld on the shortened pins would overheat the enamel. But once twisted, no pin can be changed economically, and we found no published repair for a cured winding: such a stator is most likely scrap, with its 5.8 kilograms of copper. The arithmetic is unforgiving. If one weld in a thousand is bad, almost one in ten of our stators carries one; with 200 welds, nearly one in five. For 99 good stators in 100, fewer than one weld in 10,000 may fail. So every result is stored under the stator's code.

    11:54Summary

    Summary. Four points. One. Resin fills the gaps of the winding: it fixes the pins, in our estimate more than halves the heat resistance to the steel, and keeps out moisture. Two. The stator is trickled hot and turning; capillary action fills the gaps, then the resin gels and cures. Three. Resistance shows the paths are complete, not that every weld is good: one poor weld moves our reading by 0.013 percent. Four. Insulation resistance, hipot, surge and partial discharge check the insulation, before and after the resin. With our five-slot crown, half the slot neighbours are two phases. That is the end of lecture five. Next, lecture six: into the motor, where the stator meets its housing and rotor. Until then, explore the finished stator in the free Viewer.
