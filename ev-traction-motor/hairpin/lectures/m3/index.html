    In short

    What to take from this lecture

    • The wire sets the start: soft annealed copper in a rounded rectangle; on our EV-size 3.60 × 4.45 mm bar the enamel takes about 12 % of the section.
    • Straighten, strip and cut while the bar is straight: one 18 mm stripped window, cut in the middle, gives the 9 mm bare ends of two pins.
    • Bend the U in 2D, then form the crown in 3D: a 6 mm layer change at the peak and a roll onto the bore. Formable bends (inner radius twice the bar side, 20 % strain at the outer edge) raise our crown from 37.4 to 44.3 mm.
    • Copper springs back: 0.92° on our 47° shoulder bend, 3.2 mm at the leg tip about 200 mm away, and it changes from batch to batch, so lines overbend, measure and correct.

    0:00From wire to pins

    From wire to pins. Lecture three of the Manufacturing module. Lecture two designed our winding pin by pin. Our EV-size stator, 48 slots and 8 poles, needs 96 hairpins in six shapes. Now we make them, from a reel of flat copper wire to a formed pin, ready to insert. Every pin goes through the same steps: straighten, strip, cut, bend the U, form the crown. One machine maker does them all in one cell, at one to one and a half seconds a pin: about two minutes for our stator. The shapes and lengths come from our Hairpin Motor Designer, the generator that built the stator in lecture two.

    0:48Flat wire and enamel

    Chapter one. Flat wire and enamel. The wire is pure copper, tough-pitch or oxygen-free. It is drawn, rolled or extruded into a rounded rectangle, then annealed soft, so it bends without cracking. Our EV-size pin uses a bar 3.6 millimetres wide and 4.45 high, over the enamel. Hairpin wires run from about 2 by 1.5 to 6 by 6 millimetres. The enamel is built up in many thin passes, often polyamide-imide over a polyester-imide base. Our model gives it 0.12 millimetres a side. That makes 12 percent of the bar's section insulation, not copper. For 800-volt motors some makers use extruded PEEK instead, a plastic put on in one pass; one supplier quotes 0.1 to 0.2 millimetres. The corners are rounded to radii set in the wire standard, IEC 60317. A sharp corner concentrates the electric field and is the first place the film cracks when bent. A large radius wastes slot copper. And the wire is not uniform. One study measured its stiffness, the Young's modulus, at 84 to 98 gigapascals, against 110 to 130 in handbooks, and about ten percent between batches. Chapter five shows why that matters.

    2:22Straighten, strip, cut

    Chapter two. Straighten, strip, cut. The wire comes off a reel, so it is curved. Two sets of rollers, one for each plane, bend it back and forth, less each time, until it runs straight. Setting those rollers is a skill. One machine maker says it can take an experienced operator two hours, so new lines measure the wire and set the rollers themselves. Next the enamel comes off where the welds will be: scraped by a mill or brushes, or burned off by a laser. One common recipe uses two lasers. A carbon-dioxide laser takes the bulk but leaves a film of one or two micrometres, and a second laser cleans it. Why so clean? The weld melts the copper. Any polymer left on it burns, and the gas throws out spatter and leaves pores in the weld. Lecture four comes back to this. In our model each end is bare for nine millimetres: the seven-millimetre weld tip and two below it. Then a punch cuts the wire in the middle of a stripped window. One 18-millimetre window gives the bare end of one pin and the start of the next. The punch also chamfers all four edges, so the leg slides into its slot without cutting the liner. Each shape has its own length. In our stator the cut lengths run from 449 millimetres, for the short special pin, to 492 for the long one. In all, 45.1 metres of wire and about 5.7 kilograms of copper.

    4:11Bend the U

    Chapter three. Bend the U. First the rod is bent flat, in one plane, into the U you met in lecture two: two straight legs and a pointed roof between them. On our pin the roof rises at 43 degrees. So each leg turns 47 degrees into the roof, at the shoulder, and the roof turns 86 degrees at its peak. The legs end up five slots apart, the crown pitch. Machines bend it in three ways. A die presses the whole U in one stroke. A rotary draw bender makes the bends one after another. Or a CNC or robot bender moves freely, with no tool tied to one shape. Dies are fast, but each shape needs its own: six for our stator. Free bending changes the shape in software, but it feels the wire's spread more. Every bend stretches its outer edge. The strain is t, the thickness in the bend plane, over twice the inner radius plus t. Our shoulder bends lie in the plane of the 3.6-millimetre side, so the bar bends flat, the easy way. Round an inner radius of twice that, 7.2 millimetres, the outer edge stretches 20 percent. The copper takes that easily; one supplier's flat wire breaks only beyond 32 percent. The enamel is the limit. The same data sheet bends the wire edgewise round a mandrel four times its width, with no cracks allowed: the same 20 percent stretch. Does forming harm the enamel? One study bent edgewise wire to 3.5 millimetres without cracks, and the voltage at which partial discharges start did not fall. One patent reports a 35 percent drop at the tight bend in the middle of the crown, bent and pressed in a die. So the risk sits in tight, pressed bends, and formed pins are tested, in lecture five.

    6:16Form the crown

    Chapter four. Form the crown. A flat U does not fit the stator. Its legs must go into two different layers, and its crown must follow the curve of the bore. So a second die forms it in 3D. At the peak it steps the pin out by one layer pitch, 6 millimetres on our core. This step is the layer change. It also rolls the U onto a cylinder. Our pin spans 37.5 degrees of the stator. Left flat, its middle would sit 4.2 millimetres inside the curve. On a small 24-slot, 4-pole core the span is a quarter turn and the gap 14 millimetres: fewer poles, wider pins, more curve. Lines do this in different orders: a 2D bend, then a 3D die, sometimes while a robot carries the pin to the stator; or the full 3D shape in one machine. Real bend radii cost height. Lecture two drew our crown about 38 millimetres high, with tight corners. With the shoulders bent at 7.2 millimetres, it grows to 44. The roof stays tight: the neighbouring pins cross right over it, 1.5 millimetres clear.

    7:40Springback and checks

    Chapter five. Springback and checks. When the die opens, the pin opens a little too. Bending leaves elastic strain in the copper, and when the tool lets go, that part recovers. This is springback. We calculated it for our 47-degree shoulder bend at 7.2 millimetres, using the flow curve that Wirth and colleagues measured on hairpin wire in 2024: how its strength rises as it is bent. It springs back about 0.9 degrees. That sounds small, but the leg tip is about 200 millimetres away, so it moves 3.2 millimetres. So the machine overbends by the amount the wire will spring back. But that amount moves with the wire. Make it ten percent less stiff and ten percent harder, and our bend opens 0.2 degrees more: another 0.7 millimetres at the tip. A real pin has several bends, and the errors add. In one simulation, a ten percent spread in the wire moved a pin's open end by 5.5 to 11.5 millimetres; the tolerance was under one. So the best lines close the loop: measure each pin, compare it with the target, correct the next one. In one study the first pins, set by a model, were off by 0.6 to 0.8 degrees; after one correction, by 0.13 at most. The checks: a camera in the machine for the leg positions and the stripped ends, with no enamel left on them; a 3D scanner offline, about eight minutes a pin in that study; and the enamel thickness on the incoming wire. Why so strict? A pin with a wrong pitch, a bent leg or a misshapen crown jams the insertion, and once it is in the stator there is no economic way to fix it.

    9:45Summary

    Summary. Four points. One. The wire sets the start: soft copper, a rounded rectangle, and enamel that takes 12 percent of our bar. Two. Straighten, strip and cut while the bar is straight. One stripped window, cut in the middle, gives the bare ends of two pins. Three. Bend the U in 2D, then form the crown in 3D: a layer change at the peak, and the curve of the bore. Four. Copper springs back about a degree at a bend, and differently from batch to batch, so lines overbend, measure and correct. That is the end of lecture three. Next, lecture four: insert, twist, weld, how 96 pins become one winding. Until then, look at our pins and their crowns in the free Viewer.
