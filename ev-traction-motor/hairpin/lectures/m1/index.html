Why hairpins

What a hairpin winding gains over round wire, and what it gives up.

    In short

    What to take from this lecture

    • In our slot, 130 round wires fill 0.44 and four bars fill 0.72: 32 mm² of bare copper becomes 55, so at the same current the DC loss falls 41 %.
    • Hairpin end windings are not automatically shorter: on our 24-slot core the crown stands 51.5 mm against 32 mm for round wire; the pole pitch sets the height.
    • Flat bars give the heat a short, solid path: at the same 5 W the hot spot in our slot rises a third less.
    • The catch is AC loss above about 1 kHz (our four bars: K = 3.45; eight thinner bars: 1.49), and a winding whose turns change only in steps.

    0:00Why hairpins

    Why hairpins. Lecture one of the Manufacturing module. In the sizing lectures you set a slot current density. That number hid an assumption: how much of the slot is actually copper. What did you assume? Hold that thought. Here is the same motor twice, on our own parametric model. Not a carmaker's motor: our design, same core, same slots. One in round wire, one in rectangular bars: the hairpins. Early EVs such as the 2012 Nissan Leaf used round wire. The 2010 Chevrolet Volt took hairpins to the mass market; by 2019 the Porsche Taycan and the Volkswagen MEB drive had followed. You will learn when the bars win, and what they cost.

    0:52Slot fill

    Chapter one. Slot fill. Round wire goes into the slot at random. Between the circles, between enamel and liner, there is air. Across published machines the copper is about 0.4 to 0.5 of the slot area. Rectangular bars stack flat against each other and the slot wall. Design papers with real liners and bend tolerances report about 0.65 to 0.75. Reviews quote 0.8 as a ceiling, not the norm. Watch the counter. This slot has 86.8 square millimetres. The round-wire version's 130 wires fill 0.44 of it; four bars fill 0.72. Resistance goes as one over copper area. Counting bare copper, 32 square millimetres become 55. At the same current the DC loss falls to 0.59, 41 percent lower. At the same loss, 31 percent more current. One group redesigned a heavy-duty traction motor from random wire to hairpin, same specification: continuous current density 11.3 to 14.5 amps per square millimetre, peak power density 6.1 to 7.8 kilowatts per kilogram. That is the first and biggest reason. More copper in the same iron.

    2:28End winding

    Chapter two. End winding. Copper outside the core makes no torque; it only adds resistance and mass. In one hairpin design example the end winding was up to half of the whole stator winding. A round-wire end winding is a loose bundle. A hairpin end winding is legs bent to defined angles, and on the crown side there are no joints at all. Reviews quote up to 50 percent shorter end turns. Our own model says: not automatically. On this small 24-slot core the round-wire end winding stands 32 millimetres out of the core; the hairpin crown, 51.5. On our 48-slot, 8-pole EV-size core the crown is 37.6: mainly the pole pitch, not the slot count, sets the height. Crown height is geometry. Each leg travels half the coil pitch around the bore before meeting its partner, and the legs of four layers must clear each other by 1.5 millimetres. The motor can still shrink: in one like-for-like redesign the stack went from 150 to 140 millimetres, because the slot holds more copper.

    3:45Heat path

    Chapter three. Heat path. Loss is made in the copper. It has to cross the slot to the tooth, then the iron, the housing and the coolant. The slot is the hardest step. A random slot is copper, enamel, air pockets and whatever impregnant got in, modelled with one equivalent conductivity, measured at around 0.45 to 0.54 watts per metre kelvin. A hairpin slot is modelled bar by bar: solid copper at 401 watts per metre kelvin, separated by thin, known insulation gaps. The equivalent for a flat-wire slot comes out at 1.4 to 2.8. Roughly three to five times better. On our slot in FEMM, with the same 5 watts in it, the hot spot rises 15.4 kelvin with round wire and 10.5 with bars: a third less. Impregnation matters for both. In one round-wire test motor, epoxy instead of varnish raised the slot's conductivity from 1.65 to 2.96. We come back to impregnation in lecture five.

    4:56AC loss at speed

    Chapter four. AC loss at speed. This is the catch. A solid bar does not carry alternating current evenly. Skin effect pushes it to the surface; leakage flux from neighbouring bars pushes it toward the slot opening. The bar nearest the opening suffers most. Electrical frequency is pole pairs times speed over sixty. An eight-pole motor at 12,000 rpm runs at 800 hertz; at 18,000, 1.2 kilohertz. Hairpin EV motors live here. To see it, we modelled one slot of our stator in FEMM, a free finite-element program. With four bars, at 50 hertz the current fills the copper. At one kilohertz it crowds toward the slot opening. The loss ratio K, the AC loss divided by the DC loss at the same current, rises to 3.45. Split into eight thinner bars, it drops to 1.49. A published study found the same trend on taller bars: at one kilohertz, two layers gave 11.45, eight layers 5.56. Round wire is not free of this. Strands laid at random form loops, and circulating currents dominate. In the same-motor redesign at one kilohertz the random winding's loss ratio was 1.91, the hairpin's 1.43; at 333 hertz both were about 1.1. Two more levers. Thinner pins near the opening cut the loss 28 percent at one kilohertz. And temperature: hot copper is more resistive, so the frequency where the loss doubles moved from about 650 hertz cold to about 1,200 hertz warm. The design rule: hairpins are comfortable below about one kilohertz. Above that you are into many thin layers or stranded conductors, and manufacturing gets harder.

    7:04Production and flexibility

    Chapter five. Production and flexibility. Four phases: form the pins, insert, twist, weld. Every step can be a robot or a press. One review estimates that at one million units a year, this costs up to 27 percent less than random windings. What you pay for that is welds and tooling: more than 100 laser welds per stator, seven to ten pin shapes in a 48 or 54-slot design, and tooling that fits one stator geometry only. Now the freedom you give up. One turn per coil. An even number of bars per slot, usually 4, 6 or 8. Changing the turns means changing slot count, layers or parallel paths, in steps, with new tooling. A design needing 30 or 52 turns per coil is not a hairpin design; that one was built in round wire. What you get back is certainty. Every bar sits where the plan says, so the voltage between neighbours is known and can be designed; one study cut conductor-to-ground voltage by 45 percent by shifting the winding plan. In a random winding the first and last turn of a coil can lie side by side and see up to 90 percent of the applied voltage on a 50 nanosecond edge. At 800 volts, that matters. So. Frozen design, high volume: hairpin. Low volume or many variants: round wire, on the same winding machine.

    8:47Summary

    Summary. Four points. One. Fill. On our slot, 0.44 with round wire and 0.72 with bars. More copper, lower DC loss, more current for the same iron. Two. Heat. Flat bars touch the liner; the slot conducts three to five times better, and our hot spot rises a third less. Three. The catch is AC loss past about one kilohertz. Thin layers, segmented pins and correct transposition keep it in check. Four. Hairpins buy automation and certainty, and pay in welds, tooling and fixed turn counts. That is the end of lecture one.
