    In short

    What to take from this lecture

    • Layers set the turns: our 24 slots and four layers make 48 pins and 16 turns per phase (N = layers · p · q / a); more layers mean thinner bars and a taller crown.
    • The crown spans about one pole pitch. Shortening it to 5 slots cuts the 5th and 7th harmonics from 0.259 to 0.067, lowers the crown and lengthens the weld end.
    • Special pins (7 and 5 slots), switched welds, jumpers and leads close each phase path; each is another shape and another tool. Parallel paths must sit in every layer and slot.
    • Slots and poles trade: on our core 48 slots double the pins and welds, cut the fill from 0.72 to 0.59 and nearly remove the heat advantage over round wire; more poles lower the crown and raise the frequency.

    0:00Designing a hairpin winding

    Designing a hairpin winding. Lecture two of the Manufacturing module. Lecture one showed why hairpins win. But a hairpin stator is not wound; it is assembled from rigid pins. So the winding is designed pin by pin, before any copper is cut. We use our own Hairpin Motor Designer, on the 24-slot, 4-pole core from lecture one. By the end you will read a winding diagram: layers, pitch, special pins, welds, jumpers and parallel paths.

    0:37Pins and layers

    Chapter one. Pins and layers. A hairpin is one bar bent into a U. The bend is the crown. The two legs pass through two slots and come out at the far end, where they are twisted and welded. Each slot has an even number of bar positions: the layers. We number them from the bore, layer one nearest the rotor. Our slot has four. A pin always joins two neighbouring layers: one and two, or three and four. So four layers make two layer pairs. Now count. Twenty-four slots times four layers is ninety-six bars. Each pin has two legs, so forty-eight hairpins. Each pin is one turn. So the turns per phase are the layers, times the pole pairs p, times q, the slots per pole per phase, over the parallel paths a. Here: four times two times two, over one. Sixteen. More layers, more turns: six layers give 24, eight give 32. But the bars get thinner, the fill falls from 0.72 to 0.64, and the crown grows from 51.5 to 63.4 millimetres, because more legs must twist past each other. The welds follow: 42 with four layers, 90 with eight.

    2:11Pitch and twist

    Chapter two. Pitch and twist. The pole pitch is the slots per pole: 24 over 4 is 6. For the voltages in a pin's two legs to add, the legs must sit about one pole pitch apart. So our pins span 6 slots at the crown. At the weld end each leg is bent sideways three slots: odd layers one way, even layers the other. Two legs that started six slots apart meet in the middle, ready to weld. So each pin and its weld carry the path twelve slots on: two poles. Two pins take it once round the stator. This is a wave winding. You can shorten the crown pitch to five slots. The weld side must then span seven to keep the twelve. On our core the crown drops from 51.5 to 46.4 millimetres, and the weld end grows from 77.1 to 87.4. Why do it? The winding factor is the share of the pin voltages that adds up. Short pitch costs little at the fundamental, 0.966 to 0.933, but cuts the fifth and seventh harmonics from 0.259 to 0.067. An early hairpin patent recommends about five sixths of a pole pitch. The price: some slots now hold two phases. In our model the worst voltage between neighbouring bars rises from 0.88 to 1.58 times the phase peak. The insulation has to hold that; lecture five tests it.

    3:53Special pins

    Chapter three. Special pins. With q equal to two, each phase owns two neighbouring slots under every pole. But a wave of twelve-slot steps goes once round and lands back in its own slot. The neighbour stays empty. So some pins are made one slot longer or shorter. In our design a seven-slot pin and a five-slot pin sit nested in one belt. The path steps one slot over, and the next lap fills the neighbouring slot. Patents and papers use the same trick: short and long-pitch pins, or a shorter connection at the weld side. Three more special parts close the path. Switched welds join layer two to layer three, moving the path to the next layer pair. One jumper per phase turns it back. And six leads, U1 to W2, go to the terminal box. Each special part is another shape, and another tool. Our stator needs six pin shapes, three jumpers and six leads; one published 48 and 54-slot design used seven to ten pin shapes. Jumpers also add material and a step, so designers keep them few.

    5:13Following one phase

    Chapter four. Following one phase. Open the trace for phase U. The current enters at lead U1, into slot one, layer four, at the bottom of the slot. It waves round in layers four and three, two laps: slots 1, 19, 13 and 8, then 2, 20, 14 and 7. The special pins make the one-slot step. Then a switched weld takes it down to layers two and one, where it makes the same two laps. In layer one it meets the jumper, which turns it round. It runs back through layers one and two, then three and four, and leaves at lead U2. Thirty-two bars in series: sixteen turns. The Designer checks every phase: each bar used once, the current direction right in every slot, three equal voltages 120 degrees apart. For half the voltage and twice the current, use two parallel paths. Leave out the jumper, and the forward and return halves become two branches of eight turns each. Parallel paths must be twins: each one in every layer and every slot of its belt. Otherwise their voltages differ, and current circulates between them. One study swapped two bars in a slot: at one kilohertz, the loss in one of them rose 88 percent.

    6:41Changing slots and poles

    Chapter five. Changing slots and poles. Now double the slots on the same core: 48 instead of 24, still four poles and four layers. The teeth get thinner, the slots narrower. Pins and welds double: 96 pins, 90 welds. So do the turns, to 32. To keep our sixteen, use two parallel paths. The bars shrink from 5.7 to 2.2 millimetres wide, but the liner and clearances across the slot stay 0.9 millimetres. In the wide slot that is 14 percent of the width; in the narrow one, 29. The fill falls from 0.72 to 0.59, and at the same phase current the DC copper loss rises 39 percent. The crown hardly moves: 51.5 millimetres, then 50.7. A pin still spans one pole pitch, the same arc on this bore. Change the poles instead: at eight poles the pole pitch halves, and the crown drops to 33.2. Eight poles have a price you met in lecture one. At the same speed the frequency doubles: at 12,000 rpm, four poles run at 400 hertz, eight poles at 800. We ran FEMM, the finite-element program from lecture one, on one 48-slot slot. The narrow bars fill less of the slot width, so the current crowds less: the loss ratio K, AC over DC loss, is 2.37 at one kilohertz, against 3.45 with 24 slots. And heat. At the same phase current the 48-slot hot spot rises 10.1 kelvin, against 10.5 with 24 slots. Less copper, but a shorter path to the tooth. One surprise. Put round wire in the same narrow slot, packed like our kit: the fill is 0.39, and per watt its hot spot is within two percent of the bars. The heat path is short either way. Hairpins keep their copper advantage; most of the heat advantage is gone. EV motors often use 48 slots and 8 poles, which one design guide calls the traditional choice. Their bore is set by the torque they need, which grows with the bore squared times the length. On a bore that size the slots stay wide: our EV-size version, 134 millimetres, has 4.5 millimetre slots, a fill of 0.71 and a 37.6 millimetre crown.

    9:29Summary

    Summary. Four points. One. Layers set the turns. Our 24 slots and four layers make 48 pins and 16 turns; more layers mean thinner bars and a taller crown. Two. The crown spans about one pole pitch. Shortening it trims the harmonics and the crown, and lengthens the weld end. Three. Special pins, switched welds, jumpers and leads close the path, and every one is another shape and another tool. Four. Slots and poles trade. More slots: more pins and welds, less fill. More poles: a lower crown and a higher frequency. That is the end of lecture two. Next, lecture three: from wire to pins, how flat wire becomes a formed hairpin.
