    What to take from this lecture

    0:00Insert, twist, weld

    Insert, twist, weld. Lecture four of the Manufacturing module. Lecture three made our 96 hairpins. Now they become one winding: into the slots, legs twisted, and the ends welded in pairs. Our example is still the EV-size stator, 48 slots and 8 poles. The steps: line the slots, set the pins in a nest, insert them, widen and twist the legs, weld the ends, then check, connect and coat. The angles, heights and weld positions come from our Hairpin Motor Designer, with the formed crowns of lecture three.

    0:43Liners and the nest

    Chapter one. Liners and the nest. First every slot gets its liner, folded insulation between copper and steel: often a laminate of aramid paper and polyimide film, sold as Nomex and Kapton, 0.13 to 0.2 millimetres thick. A machine creases the paper, folds it, pushes it into the slot and cuts it, leaving a few millimetres standing out at each end. One maker quotes 1.3 seconds a slot: about a minute for our 48 slots. Meanwhile the pins are set into a nest, a ring of grooves wider than the slots. The nest can be loose; the slots cannot. So each pin finds its place in the nest, and the full set, the basket, goes into the core together. The 48 pins of layers one and two form a ring. Most span five slots and overlap like roof tiles, so the last ones must go under the first. One patent parks the first few on a spacer, sets the rest, slips the last few under, then pushes the first ones home: on our ring, five, 38 and five. Then the second ring, layers three and four, the same way. In one study, automated setting took one to two seconds a pin; by hand, about ten.

    2:09Insert and widen

    Chapter two. Insert and widen. A gripper lifts the basket and lines up all 192 legs over the 48 lined slots, four legs to a slot. In our stator a 3.6-millimetre bar enters its lined slot with 0.15 millimetres to spare on each side. The chamfered leg ends find the slot, and guides hold the liner against the walls, so no leg can catch it and drag it in. The legs go in part way, then a press seats the whole basket, and a stop sets how far the legs stand out. Each leg travels about 190 millimetres: through the 120-millimetre core, and some 70 out the other side. Rejects at this step: a bent crown, scratched enamel, a leg standing too high or too low, or a liner torn or pushed out of place. On the weld side the legs now stand 61 to 76 millimetres out of the core, the even layers taller, as they have further to twist. Then ring tools widen them, moving each layer out a little to make room for the twist tool.

    3:26Twist

    Chapter three. Twist. The twist tool is a set of rings, one per layer. Each ring has a pocket at every slot, and each pocket takes one leg tip. Our stator needs four rings. Lecture two showed that with a five-slot crown, the weld side must span seven slots. That is the weld pitch. Seven does not halve, so the odd layers turn three slots one way, and the even layers four the other. Three plus four is seven: each leg meets its partner. On our 48-slot stator that is 22.5 degrees for the odd layers and 30 for the even ones. Opposite turns have a second benefit. One thesis puts each ring at hundreds to thousands of newton-metres. Turned opposite ways, their torques mostly cancel; one layer alone would push its whole torque through the core and its clamp, which can damage the laminations. As the tip swings round, the leg leans over, so the tip must also come down. In our stator the drop is 12 to 27 millimetres, less for the three-slot layers. This drop against angle is the twist curve; machines follow it with cams or servo axes. Each leg bends twice: at its base, where it leans over, and at the tip, which the pocket holds upright. We gave the base bend the gentle radius of the crown shoulders, an inner radius of 7.2 millimetres. Then springback again, calculated as in lecture three. The layer-one leg springs back about 0.9 degrees and its layer-two partner 1.1, so their tips move 0.7 and 1.1 millimetres. Partners twist opposite ways, so they spring apart sideways, along the row: about 1.8 millimetres. So the rings turn a little too far, then back off slightly before letting go. One research group's model overtwists by one degree: about 1.3 millimetres at our tips. After the twist our tips stand 49 millimetres above the core. The gentle bends cost 3.8 millimetres of height against sharp drawing corners.

    5:48Laser welding

    Chapter four. Laser welding. Our stator has 90 welds. 42 pairs in the inner row, 42 in the outer, and six switched welds that link the two rows, as in lecture two. The same core with eight layers would need 186. First each pair is clamped together. Before clamping, the partners may stand a few tenths of a millimetre apart, face to face. In a 2023 study by Omlor and colleagues, the weld tolerated gaps only up to 0.6 millimetres. Some machine makers also cut the tips to one height. That levels the scatter from twisting, though flat cut ends reflect more laser light than the chamfered ones. Why a laser? Copper carries heat away fast, so the energy must come in a short, intense burst. In the Omlor study the laser put about four and a half kilowatts into a spot 0.17 millimetres wide. Solid copper reflects most of an infrared beam and absorbs about five percent. Once it melts, a keyhole opens, a narrow channel of vapour, and the copper absorbs about 70 percent. Green light is absorbed better, but in one benchmark it welded four times slower, though with fewer pores. A scanner, a pair of fast mirrors, steers the beam from pair to pair. A camera finds each pair, then the beam runs small loops across both tips, an ellipse about 2.4 by 1.2 millimetres. In the Omlor study a weld took about 110 milliseconds: for our 90 welds, ten seconds of laser time, a little more for our thicker bar. One machine maker allows 50 to 200 milliseconds a pair. What goes wrong? Spatter: drops of molten copper thrown out, which can land on the enamel. And pores: gas trapped as the weld freezes. The gas comes from enamel left on the copper, as lecture three warned, and from oxygen in ordinary tough-pitch copper. Two fixes. In the Omlor study, oxygen-free copper cut the pore volume by 40 percent. A ring of light around the main spot keeps the keyhole open. On oxygen-free copper it cut pores by another two thirds, and the weld tolerated gaps of 0.8 millimetres. How big must a weld be? The joint must have at least the area of the conductor. Our copper is about 14 square millimetres and the joint 3.4 millimetres wide, so the weld must reach about 4.2 millimetres deep.

    8:36Check, connect, coat

    Chapter five. Check, connect, coat. One bad weld can scrap the stator, so every weld is checked. During welding, sensors watch the light from the melt. After it, a 3D sensor measures the bead, the solid drop of copper on each pair. Resistance is the electrical check. One study measured good joints at about three micro-ohms; a one-millimetre gap pushed them above eight. Then the leads. Our stator has two parallel paths per phase, so twelve ends have no partner: six join the three phase leads, and six the neutral ring, which joins the phases in a star. More laser welds: 102 joints in all. Last, the beads are coated. They are bare, live copper, 6 to 7.5 millimetres from the next pair, which may be another phase. So the stator is heated and its beads dipped into epoxy powder, which melts onto the hot copper. Some lines coat only after impregnation, in lecture five.

    9:42Summary

    Summary. Four points. One. The pins are set in a loose nest, the last under the first, then pushed into the lined slots together. Two. Odd and even layers twist opposite ways, three and four slots, so each leg meets its partner and the torques mostly cancel. Three. Springback shifts partners sideways, so the rings overtwist, and each pair is clamped before welding. Four. A laser welds each pair in about a tenth of a second. Residue and oxygen make pores, and one bad weld can scrap a stator, so every weld is checked. That is the end of lecture four. Next, lecture five: impregnate and test, how the winding is sealed and proven. Until then, insert, twist and weld our stator step by step in the free Viewer.
