    0:00Into the motor

    Into the motor. Lecture six, the last on the hairpin stator. Lecture five left our EV-size stator, 48 slots and 8 poles, impregnated and tested: 9.17 milliohms between two leads, and its insulation proven. Now it becomes a motor. Five steps: housing, cooling, connections, rotor, and a last test of the whole motor. Beside it, wherever it differs: our small 24-slot core in a finned industrial frame, an IEC frame, built as an induction motor. The numbers come from our Hairpin Motor Designer's model of that stator. It has no housing or rotor yet, so we sized both from published motors and assumed the rest; we say so each time.

    0:52Into the housing

    Chapter one. Into the housing. The housing must hold the stator for the car's life, carry its torque and take its heat. In battery cars the core is usually pressed into an aluminium housing with a water jacket; several hybrid motors bolt it in instead. The usual press is a shrink fit. The housing's bore is made slightly smaller than the core; the difference is called the interference. Heated, the aluminium grows; the cold core drops in; as it cools, the housing grips it. How much grip does the torque need? Friction at the fit must hold it. Our estimated peak torque, for an assumed rotor, is 260 newton-metres. Doubled for safety, with a friction coefficient of 0.15, that needs under half a megapascal: 0.04 millimetres of interference on our 200-millimetre core. Yet the few published fits we found are several times larger: scaled to our size, 0.12 to 0.26 millimetres. The reason is heat. Aluminium expands almost twice as much as steel, so when both warm, the fit loosens by 1.9 micrometres a kelvin. So the fit is sized for heat, not torque. We chose one that still grips with both parts soaked at 120 degrees: 0.23 to 0.28 millimetres. With a 4-millimetre aluminium wall, as in published designs, it presses about 3 megapascals at room temperature. In the car, the jacket keeps the housing cooler than the core, and the grip stays as assembled. The weak moment is a hot soak with the coolant stopped: in our model, the smallest fit lets go when both parts reach about 140 degrees. To assemble, the bore must open by the interference plus about 0.2 millimetres: in our model the housing must reach at least 131 degrees. Makers of induction heaters quote 200 to 300, in a minute or two. Could that heat harm the winding? We found no published limit. Shared evenly with core and copper, a 200-degree housing would leave them near 110 degrees, a 300-degree one near 160; the core's outside gets hotter first. The core pays too. The fit squeezes the yoke, the steel ring behind the slots: in our estimate to 25 to 31 megapascals, inside the 16 to 48 published. Compressed steel loses more: one thesis measured 18 percent more at 10 megapascals, and in its 48-slot, 8-pole model, half a point of drive-cycle efficiency.

    3:52Cooling

    Chapter two. Cooling. At 10 amps per square millimetre and 150 degrees, our winding makes 1.67 kilowatts of copper loss, half of it in the end windings, outside the core. Iron loss comes on top; without a rotor, our model leaves it out. In a water jacket, water and glycol at about 65 degrees, 6 to 10 litres a minute, run through channels in the housing around the core. Follow the heat from a slot to the water. It crosses the slot wall, that is the enamel, the resin and the liner; then tooth and yoke; then the contact between core and housing; then the aluminium, into the water. For the slot loss alone, our one-dimensional estimate puts 13 kelvin on the slot wall, 12 on the steel, 11 on the contact and 11 on the water: 47 in all. The contact costs almost as much as the slot wall, and is the least known: published models treat it as a film of still air 0.006 to 0.1 millimetres thick, which moves our 47 kelvin between 38 and 79. With the jacket alone, the end-winding heat must flow back along the copper into the slots, and in our estimate the copper reaches about 170 degrees at the far ends of the end windings. So many hairpin motors also spray oil on the end windings, about 2 litres a minute in one test. Per square metre, oil takes far less heat than water: 100 to 250 watts per kelvin, against about 1,400. But the end windings offer five times the core's outer surface, with nothing in between. Counting copper loss only, the end windings then stay near 70 degrees and the slot copper near 110. For the same 150 degrees, the winding carries half as much current again: 13.5 amps per square millimetre instead of 9. Now the slot path sets the limit. Our IEC-frame motor has no liquid: a shaft fan blows air along its fins. Even 80 kelvin above the air, they shed only about 350 watts in our estimate.

    6:24Connections and sensors

    Chapter three. Connections and sensors. Three leads leave our stator, one per phase. Each joins its phase's two parallel paths, so it carries twice a path's current. Our model draws them with the same bar as the pins, 14.15 square millimetres: when the winding carries 10 amps per square millimetre, the lead carries 20. Production stators use stamped copper bars, one per phase and one for the neutral, moulded into a plastic carrier over the weld side; we found no published cross-section. To match the winding, our leads would need 28 square millimetres. The neutral ring, by contrast, needs no more copper. Its six path ends sit in the order U, W, V, U, W, V, so no stretch of it carries more than one path's current. Where do the leads go? Our IEC-frame motor brings six leads to a box on top, with links for star or delta. The EV motor has its star inside, so three leads rise out of the end, to an axial box or straight through the wall into the inverter. Two sensors complete the stator. A thermistor, a resistor that changes with temperature, sits on the neutral ring or in the end winding. In one study the hot spot ran 50 kelvin ahead of it after a load step, so the controller adds a thermal model. And a resolver, a small rotary transformer on the shaft, tells the inverter where the rotor's poles are. For 8 poles its signal repeats four times a turn.

    8:09The rotor

    Chapter four. The rotor. The rotor and its magnets get lectures of their own; here we only fit one, sized from published motors. One teardown of a 48-slot, 8-pole motor with a core of our diameter found an air gap, the clearance between rotor and stator, of 0.89 millimetres. Others report 0.55 to 0.73. We take 0.7. Its magnets are magnetised before it goes in, and they pull on the core: B squared over two mu-nought per unit area, with B the flux density in the gap and mu-nought the permeability of air. At the 0.9 tesla we assume, 320 kilopascals. Centred, the pulls cancel; with the rotor against one side, we estimate some 5 kilonewtons, the weight of half a tonne. So the rotor never goes in by hand: a machine holds rotor and stator on one axis with guide sleeves. A touch could chip a magnet or break its coating, and one simulation finds the pull largest just as the rotor enters. Two ball bearings carry the shaft, at about 18,000 revolutions a minute in one recent car. The inverter can drive small sparks through their grease, so many motors use insulated or ceramic bearings, or a grounding ring. Once the motor is closed the gap cannot be measured, so the bearing seats must keep it even: a repair-shop rule asks within 10 percent of the mean, 0.07 millimetres for ours. Our IEC-frame motor has a die-cast cage rotor with no magnets, so there is no pull: it slides in by hand and runs in two small ball bearings.

    10:03End-of-line test

    Chapter five. End-of-line test. Every finished motor is tested again. The insulation tests of lecture five are repeated, and the sealed water jacket is filled with helium: one maker of leak testers allows about 0.06 cubic centimetres a minute to escape. Then a drive motor spins it, its own terminals open. The magnets induce a voltage, the back-EMF, growing with speed, turns and flux. At our assumed 0.9 tesla, our 16 turns should give about 28 volts r.m.s. between two leads per thousand revolutions a minute. The three phases must match each other and the design. Back-EMF finds a weak, missing or wrongly fitted magnet; in one study it hardly saw an off-centre rotor. The same spin sets the resolver: the tester shifts its zero until it lines up with the rotor's magnets, to about half a degree of the electrical cycle. Last, noise and vibration. Sensors on the housing track orders, tones at fixed multiples of the speed: our 48 slots give a 4.8-kilohertz whine at 6,000 revolutions a minute. Limits are learnt from good motors; in one study, a rotor 0.2 millimetres off centre changed single orders strongly. Our IEC-frame motor has no magnets, so no back-EMF: it runs at no load, and its current and losses are compared instead.

    11:36Summary

    Summary. Four points. One. A shrink fit holds the stator, sized for heat: in our model the torque needs only about a sixth of it. Two. Heat crosses the slot wall, the steel and the contact to the water; in our estimate, oil on the end windings adds half as much current again. Three. Each lead carries both parallel paths; a thermistor and a resolver complete the stator. Four. The magnet rotor goes in on a guide against kilonewtons of pull, by our estimate, and every motor is tested again. That is the end of lecture six, and of our lectures on the hairpin stator. Flat wire became pins, the pins a welded, impregnated stator, and the stator a motor.
