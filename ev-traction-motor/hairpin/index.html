How a hairpin stator is made

How the hairpin winding of an EV traction motor is made. Stack, insulate, insert, expand, twist, weld, coat, connect: every step of a hairpin stator on our own parametric 3D model, which you can turn, cut open and play as a build sequence. What it is, and why EV motors use it

Bars instead of wire

0.65–0.75
copper fill of the slot with hairpins, against 0.40–0.55 with random round wire. Flat bars stack against each other and the slot wall; round wires leave voids.
+28 %
continuous current density, 11.3 to 14.5 A/mm², when one heavy-duty traction motor was redesigned from round wire to hairpins; active mass −17 %, peak slot temperature about 10 °C lower.
>100
laser welds per stator, and one turn per coil with 4, 6 or 8 conductors per slot: the price of a winding that a machine can make the same way every time.

A hairpin stator carries its winding as solid rectangular copper bars instead of bundles of thin round wire. Each bar is bent into a U, the hairpin, pushed into two slots from one end of the core, twisted at the other end and welded to its neighbour. The winding is built up from the pins, one turn per coil, in an even number of layers per slot.

EV makers took it up for three reasons. The slot holds more copper, so the same slot carries more current for the same loss, or the same current with less heat. The bars sit flat against the liner and each other, so a hairpin slot conducts heat to the core at the copper itself, 401 W/(m·K), bar by bar, where a random-wound slot is modelled at an equivalent 0.45 to 0.50 W/(m·K). And every conductor is in a known place, so the voltage between neighbours can be designed: a change of winding plan cut the conductor-to-ground stress of one design by 45 %.

The costs are real too. Solid bars suffer skin and proximity effect, so above about 1 kHz the AC loss climbs; eight thin layers instead of two cut the AC-to-DC loss ratio at 1 kHz from 11.5 to 5.6. The turns per coil cannot be chosen freely, every stator needs its own pin tooling, and the welds decide the cost: at a million units a year the hairpin line is up to 27 % cheaper, at low volume it is not. Hairpins reached series cars through GM hybrids; the 2010 Chevrolet Volt took them to the mass market, and since 2018 they have been the default in new EV platforms, from the Porsche Taycan and the VW MEB drive onwards.

The build, step by step

From the lamination stack to a tested stator

Thirteen steps. Ten are shown on our parametric model, with one still from the viewer each; three are not modelled yet and have a diagram instead. The stills show the 48-slot, 8-pole, 4-layer EV-size stator: conductor 3.60 × 4.45 mm, two parallel paths, star.

01On our model

Stack the laminations

Electrical steel sheets, punched with the slots, are stacked on a mandrel with slot 1 aligned, to the stack length the design asks for. Our model shows packets arriving one after the other; a real line stacks loose sheets or pre-glued packets and compresses them to length.

02On our model

Weld the core

Weld seams along notches on the outside hold the stack together (six on our model). Other cores are held by interlocking tabs, bonding varnish or a cleated frame. Whichever way, the aim is a stiff core whose slots line up along the full length, because the hairpins go in straight.

03Not modelled yet · animation in the lecture series

From wire to pins

The hairpins start as rectangular enamelled copper wire on a reel. The wire is straightened, the enamel is stripped from the two ends that will be welded, the wire is cut to length, bent into a U, and then formed in three dimensions so that its two legs land in different slots and different layers. A stator needs several pin shapes: published designs use 3 to 16 different hairpin geometries; our 48-slot model uses 96 standard hairpins and 24 special ones for the path ends and jumpers. Every pin must be right first time: a wrong pin arrangement is fatal to the stator, and damaged enamel impairs it.

We have not modelled the forming tools yet. The lecture M3 From wire to pins adds the animation.

04On our model

Insert slot liners and closures

Before any copper goes in, each slot gets a folded liner: the phase-to-ground insulation between the pins and the laminated core. In hairpin traction stators it is aramid paper, an aramid–polyimide–aramid laminate or polyimide film, 0.1 to 0.3 mm thick depending on the material (0.125 mm polyimide film in one qualified automotive stator). A 0.25 mm aramid paper withstands about 8 kV. The ends are folded into cuffs that lock the liner in the slot and keep the pins off the lamination edges. A machine that inserts pre-formed sleeves does a whole stator in 10 to 20 s. The liner is the least critical insulation in a hairpin design; the critical one is phase to phase in the end winding, where many designs rely on air gaps and the enamel alone.

05On our model

Insert the hairpins

The pre-formed pins are loaded into a fixture in the order of the winding plan and pushed into the lined slots from the crown side, all legs straight. On our model the two legs of each pin sit five slots apart in neighbouring layers, as the winding plan says; four legs fill each slot, one per layer, and the copper takes 0.71 of the slot area.

06On our model

Expand the layers

On the weld side the legs of each layer ring are spread outwards, so that neighbouring rings have room to be twisted past each other. Our model keeps a radial gap of 1.5 mm between rings and at least 1.8 mm between legs in a ring.

07On our model

Twist

Odd and even layer rings are twisted in opposite directions, each by half the weld pitch, so every leg ends next to the leg it will be welded to. On our model the crown pitch is 5 slots and the weld pitch 6, the crown ramp is 37.8° and the weld ramp 32.8°; the crown stands 37.6 mm above the core and the weld side, with its connections, 68.2 mm. The pitch and the number of layers set this height, and with it the copper that carries no torque.

08On our model

Weld the tips

Each pair of bare tips is welded, in production by laser. A traction stator needs more than 100 welds, each about 110 ms with a 6 kW laser, with the two tips held within 0.6 to 0.8 mm of each other and 1.5 to 2.5 mm in height. Our model has 90 welds, of which the switched welds join the layer pairs. More layers mean more welds: going from four to ten layers doubles the number of welds. This is the step that decides the cost at volume, and it is why welded hairpins rarely go beyond 10 layers or 96 slots.

09On our model

Coat the weld heads

After welding the tips are bare copper a few millimetres apart, so they are insulated again. The weld side is dipped into a fluidised bed of epoxy powder that melts onto the hot copper, or the heads get a gel or resin coat; both are common for the ends and sit after impregnation in some process chains, before it in others.

10On our model

Fit jumpers and terminal bars

Jumpers join the parallel paths of each phase (two on our 48-slot model) and the phase ends are brought out to terminal tabs or bus bars. These are the special pins from step 3: longer, with their own bend, and welded like the rest.

11On our model

Connect the leads

A neutral ring joins the three phase ends in star, and the phase leads go out. In an IEC-frame motor they are flexible leads with ferrules, run to the terminal box; in an EV motor they are phase bus bars that go straight to the inverter. The wound stator is now ready for impregnation.

12Not modelled yet · animation in the lecture series

Impregnate

Resin fills the gaps between pins, liner and core. It bonds the winding against vibration, displaces air so that partial discharge starts at a higher voltage, and closes the heat path from the copper to the core: in one calibrated test, epoxy raised the radial slot conductivity to 2.96 W/(m·K) against 1.65 W/(m·K) with varnish. In trickle impregnation the stator is preheated, usually by an induction ring, to 80 to 130 °C, then turns at 5 to 40 rpm, tilted 5 to 20°, while nozzles drip low-viscosity resin onto the upper end winding; capillary action pulls it into the slots. The stator keeps turning until the resin gels, is levelled, and is cured at 140 to 170 °C for 15 min to 3 h depending on the resin. One machine maker quotes under 60 s per stator for the trickling itself. Impregnation raised the partial discharge inception voltage of test pairs by 10 to 15 %.

Dip, vacuum-pressure impregnation and full potting are the alternatives. The lecture M5 Impregnate and test adds the animation.

13Not modelled yet · animation in the lecture series

Test

Every stator is tested on an automated bench, once after welding and once after impregnation, so that a defect can still be reworked before the resin cures. The sequence, common to motor lines: four-wire resistance of the three phases, corrected to 20 °C; inductance; insulation resistance at 500 V DC; the withstand test at 2 × rated voltage + 1000 V for 60 s, or 1 s at 120 % of that in quantity production; a surge test, where a capacitor charged to about 2 × rated voltage + 1000 V is discharged into each phase pair and the three damped rings must overlap; a weak turn separates them; and, on EV lines, a partial discharge test, because an 800 V inverter puts up to about 1.9 kV peak-to-peak between two phases in the end winding. The results are compared before and after impregnation, which also checks the impregnation itself.

The lecture M5 Impregnate and test adds the animation of the bench.

Into the motor

The same winding in two machines

Both motors in the viewer carry the same 24-slot, 4-pole, 4-layer hairpin winding, so you can compare the packages. The IEC frame is there for teaching: industrial motors of this size are round-wound, and the hairpin version is longer because of its crown and weld overhangs. The EV housing shows what the winding is made for: a cooled jacket, a resolver on the shaft and bus bars straight out of the end. Open either one, press Cut housing or X-ray housing, and play the build: the stator is pressed or shrink-fitted in, the rotor follows, then the shields, cooling and terminal box.

Questions

Hairpin winding: short answers

What is a hairpin winding?

A stator winding made of solid rectangular copper bars instead of round wire. Each bar is bent into a U (the hairpin), pushed into two slots from one end of the core, then twisted and welded to its neighbour at the other end. It has one turn per coil and an even number of conductors per slot, usually 4, 6 or 8.

Why do EV traction motors use hairpin windings?

More copper in the same slot: about 0.65 to 0.75 of the slot area against 0.40 to 0.55 for random round wire, so lower DC loss and more current for the same heat. The flat bars also conduct heat to the core far better, and every conductor sits in a known place, so the voltage between neighbours can be designed (one plan change cut conductor-to-ground stress by 45 %).

What are the drawbacks of hairpin windings?

AC loss at high frequency: solid bars suffer skin and proximity effect, so above about 1 kHz the loss climbs, and designs answer with more, thinner layers. The turns per coil cannot be chosen freely, every stator needs its own pin tooling, and each stator has more than 100 laser welds.

How are hairpin windings welded?

The stripped tips of neighbouring legs are joined in pairs, usually by laser, at about 110 ms per weld; the weld heads are then coated to insulate them.

How is a hairpin stator tested?

On an automated bench, once after welding and once after impregnation: four-wire resistance, inductance, insulation resistance, the withstand (hipot) test, a surge test for turn-to-turn faults and, on 800 V platforms, a partial discharge test, because the inverter puts up to about 1.9 kV peak-to-peak between two phases in the end winding.
